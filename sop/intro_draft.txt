== INTRODUCTION ==

My research interests center on developing reliable supervision for foundation
models. A recent shift has shown how powerful it can be: instead of learning
only from human-written examples, models can now be trained with rewards that
check whether their answers are correct, and through this process they learn
to reason step by step. As training continues, they even learn on their own
to think longer before responding, which opens a second way to improve them:
spending more computation at test time. Yet this approach works best where
answers can be checked automatically, such as math and code. In many other
settings, models must rely on learned reward models, which judge outputs less
reliably and can be exploited during training. In my PhD, I hope to contribute
to this area by studying how learned reward models can give more reliable,
step-level feedback, both for training models and for selecting among their 
outputs at test time.

== RESEARCH EXPERIENCE 1: MASTER'S THESIS — TYPED PROCESS REWARD MODEL ==

I first encountered this challenge during my master's research on multimodal
medical reasoning. While studying process reward models, I became interested
in a key limitation: they can identify incorrect reasoning steps, but often
provide little insight into what caused the error. For medical image analysis,
a model must locate the correct region, recognize what it contains, and reason
from it to a diagnosis. A model might misinterpret an image or draw an
incorrect clinical conclusion from accurately identified visual evidence.
Although both lead to incorrect reasoning, they represent different types of
errors that require different forms of supervision.

To address this, I am working with Prof. Ruihong Huang at Texas A&M on
a typed process reward model that judges each reasoning step on two
separate axes: whether its visual claims are correct, and whether its
clinical inference holds given those claims. To build supervision without 
manually labeling each reasoning step, I sample reasoning chains from 
Qwen2.5-VL-7B, parse them into visual claims and inferences, check visual 
claims against available expert annotations, and evaluate clinical 
inferences against retrieved medical knowledge. If an inference relies 
on a visual claim that turns out to be wrong, I label it as dependent rather than counting it as a
separate error. To ensure the model is actually using the image, I test
whether its visual judgments retain when the image is replaced with a
blank one. I plan to use the trained model both for Best-of-N selection
among candidate reasoning chains and as a step-level reward in GRPO to
train models that reason from the image rather than from language priors.

== RESEARCH EXPERIENCE 2: BANGLA SPEECH EMOTION RECOGNITION ==

I encountered a related challenge in an earlier project on Bangla
speech emotion recognition, where labeled emotional speech is scarce.
After fine-tuning Whisper and HuBERT on a small labeled dataset, I
found that performance varied sharply across emotion classes, especially
those with few examples. To make use of a larger unlabeled corpus, I
implemented iterative pseudo-labeling, where I added the models'
high-confidence predictions to subsequent training rounds. But
confidence alone was unreliable: confidently wrong predictions
reinforced existing class biases, particularly between acoustically
similar emotions. To tackle this problem, I combined confidence filtering with class-balanced
selection. This raised macro-F1 from 61.8% to 68.4%, with the largest gains
in underrepresented classes. The experience taught me that adding more
data is not enough if the supervision itself is unreliable. What
matters is whether we can identify and control errors in the labels
we learn from, a concern I now revisit when constructing step-level
labels for reasoning.

== RESEARCH EXPERIENCE 3: DANCE MOTION FEEDBACK SYSTEM ==

My undergraduate research at Purdue gave me an early appreciation
for how much the quality of model-generated feedback depends on the
evidence available to it. I spent two years building an automated
feedback system for modern dance that compares student performances
against instructor references and generates personalized corrective
guidance. The project started with 3D motion tracking for live
theatre, where I needed fast, reliable pose estimation for multiple
dancers. YOLO-Pose with stereo depth sensing gave me that — roughly
3x faster and 70% more accurate than TensorFlow PoseNet. I classified
movements across 19 categories using a fine-tuned SkateFormer and
aligned student and instructor performances using FastDTW to measure
joint-level deviations. To turn these measurements into actionable
coaching, I used Gemini as a feedback generator. I provided it with
structured, joint-level movement deviations and prompted it to
generate corrective feedback tied to those measurements, rather than
relying on the model to identify movement errors on its own. The
work received the Best Research Talk Award at Purdue's Undergraduate
Research Conference.
